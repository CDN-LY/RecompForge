Body Recomposition Meal Plan on a Budget — High Protein Without the High Price Tag

Most budget meal plan articles list grilled chicken breast and canned salmon, then move on. Neither costs what they think it costs, and neither helps you when it's 11pm and you're on a 400-taka day. Here's what a body recomposition meal plan on a budget actually looks like when the money is real.

Tariq had been trying to hit 150 grams of protein for six weeks.

University student, shared flat, roughly 500 taka a day for all meals. He'd seen the protein targets on fitness pages and started calculating. Chicken breast at the supermarket: 650 taka per kilo. To hit 150 grams of protein from chicken alone, he'd need nearly 600 grams of raw breast daily. That's almost 400 taka — on protein alone, from one source, every single day. Before rice. Before vegetables. Before anything else.

The math didn't work. So he assumed body recomposition was for people with different bank accounts.

It isn't. But the protein sources have to change. Completely.

The Protein Math Nobody Does Before Writing a Meal Plan

The body recomposition target for protein sits at 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of bodyweight, according to the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition. For a 70kg person, that's 112 to 154 grams daily. Let's call it 140 grams as a working target — enough to support muscle protein synthesis without going obsessive.

Now run the cost per 10 grams of protein across common sources. This is the number that matters — not the price per kilo of food, but the price per 10 grams of actual protein:

Chicken breast (supermarket): approximately 12–15 taka per 10g protein. Eggs: 6–8 taka per 10g protein. Dried lentils (masoor dal): 3–4 taka per 10g protein. Dried chickpeas (chola): 4–5 taka per 10g protein. Canned tuna (local brand): 9–11 taka per 10g protein. Whole milk (pasteurised): 10–12 taka per 10g protein. Soya chunks (TVP): 2–3 taka per 10g protein.

The gap between chicken breast and soya chunks is not small. It's a 5x cost difference for the same protein output. A body recomposition meal plan on a budget isn't built around premium protein sources. It's built around cheap protein sources used intelligently. *(See: Protein intake for body recomposition)*

Cheap Protein Sources That Actually Work for Recomposition

These aren't compromise foods. They're the backbone of the meal plan.

Eggs
One large egg carries 6 grams of protein and costs roughly 10–12 taka. Buy them by the tray — 30 eggs for 300 taka — and the math gets better. Four eggs at breakfast alone delivers 24 grams of protein. Whole eggs also carry fat-soluble vitamins and choline that matter for hormonal function during a body recomposition phase, per research published in Nutrients. Don't throw out the yolk to save imaginary calories.

Soya chunks (textured vegetable protein)
100 grams dry weight yields about 52 grams of protein. Price: roughly 60–80 taka per 250g bag. Hydrate them in warm salted water for 20 minutes, squeeze dry, cook in any curry base. The texture mimics meat well enough that most people adapt within a week. Soy protein quality is rated comparable to animal protein on the PDCAAS scale, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization's protein quality assessment framework. The price-to-protein ratio is simply unbeatable.

Masoor dal and chola
100 grams dried masoor dal has roughly 25 grams of protein. Cooked, it becomes 250 grams and still carries that same protein. A kilo of dried masoor costs 90–120 taka. The amino acid profile lacks sufficient leucine as a standalone source, which matters for muscle protein synthesis — pair it with rice, egg, or any animal protein across the day and the issue disappears. Don't eat dal alone and call it a protein meal. Pair it.

Curd (dahi) and whole milk
250ml whole milk = roughly 8 grams of protein, approximately 30–35 taka. Homemade curd from that same milk concentrates protein slightly. Not a high-protein standalone. Useful as a protein topper — in a smoothie with a banana, stirred into a meal, added to a post-workout snack — that pushes daily totals upward without a separate meal. *(See: Best macros for body recomposition)*

Whole chicken (not breast)
When you do buy chicken, buy the whole bird or thighs on the bone. Per kilogram of actual protein content, it costs 30–40% less than breast fillet. Thigh meat has a slightly higher fat content which is irrelevant for recomposition if you're tracking overall macros. Bone-in thighs roasted in a tray need seven minutes of prep time. Skinless breast needs more cooking attention to not dry out and costs more for the privilege.

A Real Budget Day of Eating — ~140g Protein, ~350 Taka

This is for a 70kg person targeting around 1,900 calories for body recomposition. Adjust portions up or down based on your calorie target.

Breakfast (approx. 70 taka)
4 whole eggs scrambled in minimal oil: 24g protein, ~300 calories. 2 slices of bread or a small roti: 6g protein, ~150 calories. Total: 30g protein, ~450 calories.

Lunch (approx. 90 taka)
70g dry soya chunks cooked in onion-tomato masala: 36g protein. 150g cooked rice: 3g protein, ~200 calories. 1 cup masoor dal: 12g protein, ~150 calories. Total: ~51g protein, ~600 calories.

Evening snack (approx. 40 taka)
200ml whole milk + 1 banana: 8g protein, ~200 calories.

Dinner (approx. 120 taka)
150g chicken thigh (bone-in, cooked weight roughly 100g meat): 25g protein. 150g cooked rice: 3g protein. Mixed vegetables (whatever is cheap that week — cabbage, potato, gourd): 3–5g protein, ~100 calories. Total: ~31g protein, ~550 calories.

Day total: approximately 140g protein, 1,900 calories, 320–350 taka. This is not an aspirational budget. It is a real one that a student or junior-level office worker can sustain.

Honestly, the soya chunks are the unlock most people haven't found yet. Master one good soya curry and the budget protein problem mostly solves itself.

Budget Carbs and Fat That Pull Their Weight

Protein gets all the attention in body recomposition. But carbs fuel training, and fat supports hormones. Both need to be covered — cheaply. *(See: Body recomposition: calorie deficit or maintenance?)*

Rice is the best budget carb on this list. Uncontroversially. A kilo of loose rice costs 55–70 taka and yields roughly 10 cups cooked. It digests cleanly, fuels workouts, stores easily, and pairs with literally every protein source mentioned above. The glycaemic index debate is irrelevant at a budget level — carbs working is more important than carbs being theoretically optimised. Roti (whole wheat) is second. Oats are a useful breakfast carb if you find them cheap at your local grocer.

For fat, eggs are already doing work. Cooking oil (mustard or sunflower) used sensibly covers the rest. Avoid buying nuts as a primary fat source at budget level — they're expensive per calorie and disappear too fast. A small amount of coconut or mustard oil in daily cooking at 10–15ml per meal is enough to keep dietary fat above the 0.6g/kg floor that prevents hormonal suppression.

Don't overthink the fat. It mostly takes care of itself when you're eating eggs daily and cooking in oil.

The Meal Prep Reality for a Budget Recomp Kitchen

Meal prepping on a tight budget has two non-negotiable habits. Everything else is optional.

First: cook protein in bulk twice a week. Soya chunks hydrate and cook in 25 minutes. Make a large pot, refrigerate, reheat across three days. Boiled eggs take 10 minutes and keep for five days unpeeled in the fridge. Dal made in a pressure cooker lasts four days. These three alone cover most of your weekly protein without daily cooking time.

Second: buy vegetables based on what's cheap that week, not what a recipe requires. Seasonal vegetables in Bangladesh — lau (bottle gourd), begun (brinjal), potol (pointed gourd), cabbage — cycle through being the cheapest item in the market. They add volume, micronutrients, and fiber without meaningfully affecting the protein or calorie math. Trying to buy specific vegetables for a pre-planned recipe on a tight budget is where meal plans fall apart. Buy what's cheap. Cook it with your pre-made protein. Eat. *(See: Body recomposition without tracking calories)*

One digital kitchen scale (600–800 taka, one-time purchase) removes most of the guesswork. Weigh soya chunks before hydrating. Weigh rice before cooking. Weigh chicken after deboning. These three numbers tracked even loosely are enough to know whether you're hitting the protein and calorie ballpark. Without it, you're estimating — and most people estimate 30% less food than they're actually eating.

Mistakes That Kill the Budget Recomp Meal Plan

The biggest one: treating protein supplements as essential before fixing the food. A 1kg whey protein tub costs 2,500–4,000 taka and lasts 30 days at one scoop daily. That same money buys 25kg of eggs, which delivers more total protein, more micronutrients, more satiety, and more cooking flexibility. Whey is a convenience product, not a foundation. Build the food base first.

Eating out at lunch because meal prep felt like too much effort. One restaurant meal — even a simple rice plate — can run 120–200 taka and deliver far less protein per taka than anything you cook. The math is not close. Two restaurant lunches per week, rather than packed meals, can add 1,000–1,400 taka per month to the food budget while simultaneously reducing protein intake. *(See: Body recomposition not working)*

Buying "diet" or "low-fat" labelled foods. Low-fat curd, lite milk, reduced-fat anything — these products cost more and deliver less satiety per taka. Full-fat versions are cheaper, more filling, and, at budget calorie levels, not a problem. The fat content in full-fat curd eaten once a day is not what's blocking body recomposition. Insufficient protein and inconsistent training are.

The body recomposition meal plan on a budget works when the cheap, high-protein foods get taken seriously — not used as a fallback while waiting to afford "proper" nutrition. Eggs, soya chunks, dal, and whole chicken aren't the poverty version of a recomp diet. They're the efficient version. The expensive sources are a convenience premium. Nothing more.

Budget Protein Quick Reference: Soya chunks (~2–3 taka/10g protein) → Masoor dal (~3–4 taka/10g protein) → Eggs (~6–8 taka/10g protein) → Tuna (~9–11 taka/10g protein) → Chicken thigh (~10–12 taka/10g protein) → Chicken breast (~12–15 taka/10g protein). Build your body recomposition meal plan on a budget from the left side of that list, not the right.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you build muscle on a tight budget?

Yes — and the research on muscle protein synthesis doesn't require expensive protein. What it requires is adequate total daily protein (roughly 1.6–2.2g per kilogram of bodyweight) and progressive resistance training. Soya chunks, eggs, and dal can deliver that protein at a fraction of the cost of chicken breast or whey. The muscle doesn't know what the protein cost per gram was. It responds to the amino acids, not the price tag.

Is soya protein as good as chicken protein for body recomposition?

For most practical purposes, yes. Soy protein is classified as a complete protein with a PDCAAS score of 1.0, the same ceiling as casein and egg white. Studies comparing soy to whey protein for muscle gain show broadly equivalent outcomes when total protein intake is matched. The one nuance: soya chunks are slightly lower in leucine than chicken, so combining them with a small amount of animal protein or dairy across the day closes any theoretical gap.

How many eggs per day is okay for body recomposition?

For most healthy adults, three to four whole eggs daily sits well within normal dietary cholesterol ranges and is not associated with increased cardiovascular risk, according to a review in the American Heart Association's Circulation journal. If eggs are your primary cheap protein, four per day is a reasonable and economical intake. Going beyond six per day is unusual; at that point diversifying protein sources is sensible anyway for variety and micronutrient coverage.

What's the cheapest way to hit 150g protein per day?

Roughly: 70g dry soya chunks (36g protein) + 4 eggs (24g protein) + 1 cup cooked dal (12g protein) + 100g cooked chicken thigh or canned tuna (25g protein) + 250ml milk (8g protein) = approximately 105g protein from the first four sources, around 165g total. Total daily cost of those protein sources: 200–250 taka depending on local prices. The rest of the budget covers rice, vegetables, and oil.

Do I need protein powder for body recomposition on a budget?

No. Protein powder is a convenient supplement for people who struggle to hit protein targets from whole food alone — usually due to time, appetite, or preference constraints. On a budget, the same money buys more total protein from whole food sources. If you're already hitting 140–150g daily from eggs, soya, dal, and occasional meat, there is no gap for protein powder to fill. Buy the kitchen scale instead. *(See: Body recomposition for beginners)*
